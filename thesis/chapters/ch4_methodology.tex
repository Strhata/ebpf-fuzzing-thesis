\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

This thesis is structured around three research questions.

\textbf{RQ1.} Can a 1.5-billion-parameter code language model learn to generate
syntactically and semantically valid eBPF programs through supervised fine-tuning
on a labeled dataset of program--verifier-log pairs collected from the BPF
verifier?

\textbf{RQ2.} Can a GRPO reinforcement-learning loop with live KCOV kernel PC
coverage as the reward signal increase verifier path coverage beyond what the SFT
model achieves at inference time?

\textbf{RQ3.} What failure modes arise in GRPO training with live kernel feedback,
and how can they be diagnosed and systematically addressed?

RQ1 is answered affirmatively, but as a \emph{prerequisite rather than a result}:
the SFT model learns to emit valid BPF (Chapter~\ref{ch:results},
Section~\ref{sec:results-sft}), yet validity alone discovers nothing --- a model
that emits a single trivial valid program scores a near-perfect pass-rate and
explores no verifier paths. Validity is necessary, not sufficient; it is a
stepping stone to RQ2, not an end in itself.

RQ2 is the central empirical question, and it remains \emph{open}. Neither RL run
reached the scale required to decide it: run~2 executed only $\approx$200 steps
--- roughly $40\times$ shorter than run~1's 8{,}370 --- so whether a KCOV-reward
GRPO loop can push coverage \emph{beyond} the SFT model's ceiling is undetermined.
What the experiments do establish is the \emph{obstacle} in the way of an
affirmative answer (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

RQ3 is the analytical contribution of this thesis. Two failure modes are
diagnosed: GRPO gradient starvation, which is identified from training logs and
fixed by the reward redesign (Section~\ref{sec:reward-redesign}); and the
diversity / mode-collapse wall, which is characterized but remains open.

\section{Data Collection: Instrumenting Buzzer}
\label{sec:buzzer-data}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

The SFT dataset requires a large corpus of BPF programs paired with their
verifier-log outputs.
No such public corpus existed; this work collects one by instrumenting buzzer at
the kernel FFI boundary.

Buzzer is a Go binary that generates eBPF programs using one of two built-in
strategies and submits each program to the kernel via a function called
\texttt{ValidateEbpfProgram}, which wraps the \texttt{BPF\_PROG\_LOAD} system
call and returns the verifier verdict and log.
The \texttt{coverage\_based} strategy additionally opens the KCOV device and
arms tracing before each \texttt{BPF\_PROG\_LOAD}.

Buzzer is used in this work purely as a \emph{program generator and data source}:
its generated programs and their verifier verdicts are the raw material for the
SFT corpus. How buzzer uses its own KCOV instrumentation internally is outside the
scope of this thesis, and no claim is made about it; the comparison this thesis
draws is one of generation method (Chapter~\ref{ch:related-work}), not of buzzer's
internals.

To produce the training corpus, the file
\texttt{fuzzing/buzzer/pkg/units/ffi.go} was modified (approximately 50 lines)
to intercept every call to \texttt{ValidateEbpfProgram} and write a JSONL record
containing the bytecode in hexadecimal, the full verifier log, the
accept/reject verdict, and (where present) the error line and error reason.
Records are appended to a file on a virtio-9p share visible from the host.

The collection infrastructure consists of a single QEMU virtual machine running
a Linux~6.8 kernel compiled with KASAN only (\texttt{bzImage\_kasan}), managed
by a Docker container that auto-restarts the VM on crash.
Collection ran for approximately one week and produced roughly 2~million
(bytecode, verifier-log) pairs.
The RL reward oracle (Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}) boots the \emph{same} 6.8
kernel source tree with KCOV additionally enabled (\texttt{bzImage\_kasan\_kcov})
on the same Debian~trixie image, so the verifier accept/reject verdict is expected
to be identical between the corpus the model is trained on and the oracle it is
later scored against; the two builds differ only by the presence of KCOV
instrumentation, which does not change the verifier's decision logic. This identity
rests on the shared source tree rather than on a differential check, which was not
run.

\section{SFT Pipeline Design}
\label{sec:sft-design}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Dataset Curation}
\label{sec:dataset}

The 2-million-entry raw corpus has a highly skewed error-class distribution:
a small number of verifier error types (such as ``invalid access to map value'')
account for the majority of rejected programs, while dozens of rarer error classes
are represented by only a handful of examples each.
Training on the raw distribution would bias the model toward generating programs
that trigger the most common errors and would provide little signal for the
rarer, potentially more interesting error conditions.

The curation procedure applies three steps.
First, verifier error text is normalised: numeric addresses, instruction offsets,
and register values are masked to canonical tokens so that distinct error messages
that differ only in runtime-specific numbers are mapped to the same class label.
This grouping exposes the underlying error taxonomy, yielding 26 distinct error
classes.
Second, a per-class cap of 2\,000 samples is applied to the rejected-program
entries, preventing any single error class from dominating the training
distribution.
Third, all 13\,153 valid programs are retained without capping: because valid
programs are already a minority in the raw corpus, including them in full is
necessary to give the model sufficient exposure to the accepted-program
distribution that the SFT objective asks it to learn.

The final dataset contains 27\,514 examples: 13\,153 valid programs and 14\,361
invalid programs spread across the 26 error classes.
The dataset is split 90/10 for training and evaluation.
It is published on HuggingFace as \texttt{Strhata/ebpf-corpus} and serves as the
SFT training corpus for this thesis.

\subsection{Representation: Assembly Format vs.\ Raw Bytecode}
\label{sec:format-decision}

BPF programs can be represented at two levels: as raw binary bytecode
(8-byte little-endian structs, one per instruction) or as verifier-log assembly
(the textual disassembly that the kernel's verifier log prints).
The choice of representation for the LLM's output vocabulary is consequential for
training quality and is justified here from first principles.

Raw bytecode is an opaque byte sequence with no natural-language grounding.
Each byte position within an instruction struct carries a different semantic role
--- opcode, destination register packed with source register, 16-bit signed
offset, 32-bit immediate --- but this role is invisible from the token itself.
A language model tokenizing raw bytes would need to learn these positional
semantics entirely from the statistical structure of the training data, with no
character- or word-level surface cues to anchor the learning.

Verifier-log assembly makes these semantics explicit.
Each instruction is a human-readable line of the form \texttt{N: (XX) text},
where \texttt{N} is the instruction index, \texttt{XX} is the two-hex-digit
opcode byte, and \texttt{text} is a natural-language-style mnemonic with named
register operands.
Register names (r0--r9 for general-purpose registers, r10 for the frame pointer)
match the identifiers that appear in the accompanying verifier-log error messages,
making the connection between instruction operands and verifier error text
transparent at the token level.
Jump targets are expressed as relative offsets labelled by the verifier
(\texttt{+N} or \texttt{-N}), which carry instruction-level semantic content that
an absolute bytecode offset does not.

Two further practical advantages reinforce the choice.
The verifier-log format is the exact output of buzzer's data extraction path,
requiring no intermediate conversion at training time.
And because the format includes the opcode byte \texttt{(XX)} explicitly, the
pure-Python encoder described in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} can reconstruct
the raw bytecode from model output deterministically, without depending on an
assembler or a clang installation.

An example of the format as it appears in model output is:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Verifier-log assembly format --- five instructions}]
0: (b7) r1 = 0
1: (63) *(u32 *)(r10 -4) = r1
2: (bf) r2 = r10
3: (07) r2 += -4
4: (95) exit
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Reinforcement Learning Design}
\label{sec:rl-design}
% --------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Reward Function v1}
\label{sec:reward-v1}

RL run~1 uses GRPO with $G = 4$ completions per prompt, KL penalty
$\beta = 0.01$, and a maximum completion length of 600 tokens.
These hyperparameters reflect the memory budget of the local training GPU
(RTX~4070 Laptop, 8.59~GB VRAM): larger $G$ or longer sequences exhaust memory
before a full forward pass.

The reward function assigns a scalar score to each generated program after it has
been submitted to the \texttt{kcov\_validator} binary running in the KCOV-instrumented
VM.
Five tiers are defined, in order of priority:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Reward tiers for RL run~1}
\label{tab:reward-v1}
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
Tier & Condition & Reward \\
\midrule
Crash or timeout   & SSH timeout; VM may have crashed                & 2.0 \\
New kernel PCs     & ACCEPTED and at least one PC not in frontier    & 1.0 \\
Valid, no new PCs  & ACCEPTED, all PCs already in frontier           & 0.1 \\
Verifier rejected  & REJECTED by the verifier (any reason)           & 0.1 \\
Encode failure     & Output is not parseable BPF assembly            & 0.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The crash/timeout tier is assigned the highest reward because an SSH timeout
during program load suggests the VM crashed, which is the strongest evidence of
a verifier defect.
New kernel PCs are the primary exploration signal: reaching a code path not seen
in any previous batch is rewarded at 1.0.
The ``valid, no new PCs'' and ``verifier rejected'' tiers share the same value
(0.1) deliberately: rejection is informative about the program's syntax, not
about exploration quality, and penalising rejection as a hard negative signal
would push the model away from programs that stress verifier error paths ---
exactly the programs of interest.
Encode failures receive zero reward because the model output is unparseable and
provides no coverage information.

The cumulative coverage frontier is maintained as a Python set of kernel PC
addresses, updated after each batch of $G \times B$ programs (where $B$ is the
number of prompts per batch).
A snapshot of the frontier is taken before each batch is evaluated so that all
$G$ completions for a given prompt compete against the same baseline, independent
of evaluation order within the batch.

\subsection{Root Cause: \texttt{reward\_std} Collapse under Prompt-Anchored Acceptance}
\label{sec:rl-plateau}

Run~1 executed 8,370 training steps.
Cumulative new-PC growth ceased at approximately step~1,300, after which the
model made no further progress despite 7,000 additional steps of compute.
The per-step training logs show that \texttt{reward\_std}, computed per batch as
the standard deviation of the $G$ rewards, dropped to exactly zero from
step~$\approx$1,300 onward.

A zero \texttt{reward\_std} is a definitive indicator of training collapse.
The GRPO advantage for completion~$i$ is
$A_i = (r_i - \bar{r}) / \sigma_r$ (Equation~\ref{eq:grpo-advantage}).
When $\sigma_r = 0$ --- which occurs when all $G$ completions in a group receive
identical rewards --- every advantage is zero regardless of the reward values,
the policy gradient is identically zero, and no parameter update occurs.
The model is frozen in place while the training loop continues consuming compute
and GPU time.
This condition, known as gradient starvation, is analysed formally
in~\cite{grpo-starvation}.

The mechanism that drove every group to identical rewards was a prompt-anchored
generation distribution meeting a saturating coverage frontier.
The SFT training data paired each program with a \texttt{Status:~VALID} header, and
the same header was reused verbatim as the RL prompt; under it the model generated
programs the verifier accepted in 99.6\% of completions.
Once the coverage frontier stopped growing --- at approximately step~1,300 ---
essentially every completion was an accepted program that reached no
previously-unseen PC, a case the v1 reward (Table~\ref{tab:reward-v1}) scores at a
flat 0.1.
With all $G$ completions in a group scoring 0.1, the within-group variance was zero
and the gradient vanished.
The collapse was therefore a property of the reward's coarse top tier under
near-universal acceptance, not of any single rejected-program class.

A second, latent defect in the \texttt{kcov\_validator} binary compounded the
design but was not the operative cause in run~1.
\texttt{readPCs()} was called only on the \texttt{ACCEPTED} branch, so every
REJECTED program returned an empty PC set and was indistinguishable to the reward
function.
In run~1 this touched almost nothing: rejected programs were 0.3\% of completions,
far too few to move the batch variance.
The defect becomes load-bearing only once rejection is common --- the regime run~2
enters after the prompt header is removed
(Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, Section~\ref{sec:reward-validation}), where rejected
programs dominate and an empty trace would again flatten the reward.
The redesign below addresses both ends: it reads the trace unconditionally so
rejected programs carry differentiated depth, and it replaces the flat
accepted-program tier with a novelty term so that accepted programs no longer
collapse to a single value once the frontier saturates.

The fix makes trace collection unconditional --- the KCOV PCs are read for every
load attempt rather than only for accepted programs --- so both ACCEPTED and
REJECTED programs return their full trace and within-group variance is restored.

\subsection{Reward Redesign: Validity-Gated Novelty with a Soft Floor}
\label{sec:reward-redesign}

The diagnosis in Section~\ref{sec:rl-plateau} identifies the structural
requirement for GRPO training to proceed: the reward function must produce
non-zero within-group variance $\sigma_r^2 > 0$ across the group compositions the
policy actually generates --- whether the group is dominated by accepted programs,
as in run~1, or by rejected ones, as in run~2's lower-acceptance regime.
The v1 reward fails this requirement at both ends.
It collapses an all-accepted group to a flat 0.1 once the frontier saturates
(every accepted program reaching no new PC), and it collapses an all-rejected group
to a flat 0.1 as well, since REJECTED and accepted-but-no-new-PC programs share the
same value.
In either case the $G$ rewards are equal and the gradient vanishes.

\paragraph{A rejected alternative: the verdict-blind reward.}
The most direct fix is to remove the accept/reject verdict from the reward
entirely and score each program purely by the depth of its KCOV trace plus a
discovery bonus. This guarantees within-group variance --- a deeper trace earns a
higher score whether or not the program is accepted --- and it was the first
redesign implemented. It is, however, the wrong objective. \emph{Validity is a
prerequisite for finding a verifier bug}: a rejected program proves the verifier
did its job and is not a finding, whereas a bug is reached only along paths that
an \emph{accepted} program exercises. A verdict-blind reward treats a deep but
rejected program as equivalent to a valid one, and so optimizes coverage that
cannot, by construction, lead to the outcome of interest. This mirrors how a
coverage-guided fuzzer's corpus advances its frontier only on inputs that pass the
gate. The verdict-blind reward was therefore superseded before any full run.

\paragraph{The validity-gated soft floor.}
The redesigned reward first applies an \emph{eligibility gate} --- output must
encode to valid bytecode and clear a 15-instruction length floor --- and then
sorts every program that clears the gate into one of three strictly ordered
outcomes. A program with \emph{no usable signal} scores zero; a \emph{rejected but
informative} program earns graded partial credit --- the \emph{soft floor}; an
\emph{accepted} program earns a full validity bonus plus a novelty term. The
ordering is the point: among gate-eligible programs an accepted program always
outscores any rejected one, so the policy is still pulled toward validity, while
the partial credit given to rejected programs is what keeps the within-group
reward variance from collapsing. The gate is applied before the verdict, so a
sub-floor program is scored zero whether or not the verifier would have accepted
it; the strict accepted-over-rejected ordering is therefore a statement about
programs that clear the floor. In RL run~2 this is not a binding edge case ---
SFT-2's median program is 30 instructions (Section~\ref{sec:results-sft2}), well
above the floor --- but the gate must be stated explicitly for the ordering
guarantee to hold exactly.

Let $|\mathrm{pcs}|$ be the number of distinct kernel PCs that
\texttt{kcov\_validator} records while the verifier processes the program, and let
$M$ be the largest such count seen so far --- a running maximum that normalises the
floor into $[0,1]$. Let $\nu_g(p)$ and $\nu_G(p)$ be the within-batch and
persistent novelty of program~$p$, defined below. The reward is:
\begin{equation}
  r(p) =
  \begin{cases}
    0 & \text{no usable signal},\\[2pt]
    w_{\mathrm{rej}} \cdot \min\!\left(1, \dfrac{|\mathrm{pcs}|}{M}\right)
      & \text{REJECTED --- the \emph{soft floor}},\\[8pt]
    w_{\mathrm{val}} + w_{g}\,\nu_g(p) + w_{G}\,\nu_G(p)
      & \text{ACCEPTED}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:reward-v2}
\end{equation}

The three branches read as follows.
The \emph{zero} branch covers every program that carries no exploration
information: its output does not encode to valid bytecode, or it is shorter than a
15-instruction floor (too small to exercise the verifier --- the floor stops the
model from farming easy soft-floor credit with near-empty programs), or the load
attempt could not be measured at all because of an infrastructure failure (an SSH
timeout or VM error; see the note on crashes below).
The \emph{soft floor} scores a rejected program by how deep the verifier walked
before rejecting it: $|\mathrm{pcs}|/M$ is the fraction of the deepest trace yet
seen that this program reached, capped at the ceiling $w_{\mathrm{rej}}$. A program
the verifier throws out immediately scores near zero; one it rejects only after
analysing almost all of it scores near $w_{\mathrm{rej}}$. This graded credit is
what distinguishes otherwise-identical rejected programs and so holds
$\sigma_r > 0$.
The \emph{accepted} branch pays a flat validity bonus $w_{\mathrm{val}}$ plus the
novelty terms, which reward reaching \emph{rarely visited} PCs.
$\nu_g(p)$ averages $1-\mathrm{freq}/n_{\mathrm{acc}}$ over $p$'s PCs within the
current batch, so a PC hit by few of the batch's accepted programs is worth more;
$\nu_G(p)$ averages $1/(1+\mathrm{freq}_{G})$ against a decayed PC-frequency map
kept across the whole run, so a PC rarely hit over the entire run is worth more and
the decay lets long-abandoned regions become rewarding again.

By construction $w_{\mathrm{rej}} < w_{\mathrm{val}}$ (defaults
$w_{\mathrm{rej}}=0.3$, $w_{\mathrm{val}}=1.0$, $w_g=1.0$; $w_G$ is enabled at
$2.0$ for the global-novelty phase), so the gate holds exactly: the best possible
rejected program still scores below the weakest accepted program that clears the
length floor.

\paragraph{Crashes and infrastructure failures.}
A load attempt that returns nothing --- because the VM crashed or because the SSH
connection dropped --- is scored zero, uniformly across the affected batch. This
reverses the v1 design (Table~\ref{tab:reward-v1}), which gave such timeouts the
highest score, 2.0, on the assumption that a timeout marked a kernel crash and so a
verifier defect. The two causes are indistinguishable at the reward layer: a
crashed VM and a dropped connection both return nothing, so the v1 rule rewarded
transient infrastructure noise as highly as a genuine bug --- a channel the policy
could exploit without ever stressing the verifier. Genuine crashes would instead be
recovered from the KASAN and kernel-panic instrumentation at the VM
(Section~\ref{sec:buzzer-data}), where a real defect leaves a dump that a timeout
does not. Crash detection therefore sits outside the reward entirely, with the
component that can confirm it.

\paragraph{Restoring within-group variance.}
The redesign breaks the two degeneracies that flatten a group's rewards, one per
regime. In run~1's near-universal-acceptance regime the group is all
\emph{accepted}: under the v1 reward every member scored a flat 0.1 once the
frontier saturated, so the variance vanished. The novelty term removes this
degeneracy --- two accepted programs that walk different PCs earn different
$\nu_g$, so $\sigma_r > 0$ even when no globally-new PC appears. In run~2's
$\approx$7\% accept regime (the rate measured once the prompt header is removed) the
harder case is the all-\emph{rejected} group, which arises for roughly a third of
groups at $G=16$ ($0.93^{16}\approx0.31$): under v1 every member scored the same flat
0.1, whereas under the soft floor they spread across a continuum of
depth-proportional scores, so $\sigma_r > 0$ holds even before a single valid program
appears.

The guarantee is conditional, and the condition is worth stating precisely. Reward
variance is non-zero whenever a group contains at least two gate-eligible programs
that differ in novelty (the all-accepted case) or in trace depth (the all-rejected
case); the novelty weighting additionally carries the exploration pressure intended
to counter the diversity collapse characterized in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Two
degenerate compositions still flatten the variance: a group in which \emph{every}
completion lands in the zero branch --- all encode-failures, all sub-floor, or a
batch-wide infrastructure failure --- and a group of rejected programs that all
reach \emph{identical} depth. Neither is structurally excluded; both are
empirically negligible at $G=16$ (an all-encode-failure group is
$\approx 0.25^{16}$ at the observed encode-failure rate, and exact depth ties across
a whole group are vanishing once $M$ is large). The redesign therefore holds
$\sigma_r > 0$ across the group compositions the policy produces with non-negligible
probability, rather than across every composition that can in principle occur --- a
weaker statement than a universal guarantee, but the one the data and the reward's
zero branch actually support.

The reward is implemented in \texttt{ml/reward.py} and served to the
(Colab-hosted) trainer over HTTP by \texttt{tools/reward\_server.py}. RL run~2 uses
$G=16$ (larger groups raise the chance of $\geq 1$ accepted program per group),
maximum completion length 512, $\beta = 0.05$, learning rate $5\times10^{-6}$, and
sampling temperature 0.9. Its results are reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, Section~\ref{sec:run2}.
